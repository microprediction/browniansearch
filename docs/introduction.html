Introduction

Brownian landscapes came from economics, the same object with large budgets is studied in optimization, and few-shot search sits between them.

Search on an unknown landscape

A firm chooses a strategy, a designer a parameter, an engineer a location. Each choice has a performance, and performance varies with the choice in a way nobody knows in advance. Nearby choices perform similarly, and distant ones may perform very differently. That unknown function is the landscape.

Trying a choice reveals its performance at that point only. A searcher who can afford a few trials must decide where to try next and, at the end, where to commit.

To reason about this one needs a model of how the landscape varies. The simplest is to treat performance along a line of choices as the path of a random process: Brownian motion, or a process that reverts toward an average. The literature below uses such models in three ways: to explain how organizations search, to design optimization algorithms, and to describe landscapes in nature.

Brownian landscapes in economics

Callander (2008) introduced the Brownian policy landscape: the outcome of a policy is an unknown function of its position, drawn from a Brownian motion. In that paper, delegation to a biased expert survives only when the bias is small relative to the landscape's volatility.

The 2011 papers use the landscape for search (Callander 2011a; Callander 2011b). An agent experiments when the incumbent outcome falls below a threshold and stops otherwise. Search ends almost surely, often at a mediocre policy.

In that model a sequence of short-lived agents each makes one trial, so search is myopic by construction. Later papers derive similar thresholds for precedent (Callander and Clark 2017), risk-averse learning (Callander and Matouschek 2019) and agenda control (Callander and McCarty 2024). In all of them the number of evaluations is endogenous and payoffs accrue every period.

Bardhi and Callander (forthcoming) survey the line. Two-period foresight is understood, and farsighted search by a long-lived agent with a free choice of location is largely open.

Small budgets and farsighted searchers

Two papers treat very few evaluations. Callander and Hummel (2014) analyze a two-period game in which each of two policymakers can experiment once. Ganz (2020) studies an organization that allows a manager one offline trial before an online choice, so only the second evaluation is paid.

Other papers obtain farsighted results under other restrictions:

  • contiguous search, with a closed-form drawdown stopping rule (Urgun and Yariv 2025), extended to teams (Cetemen, Urgun and Yariv 2023) and to flow payoffs (Wong 2025);
  • choosing several sample locations at once (Bardhi 2024);
  • infinite sequences of short-lived agents (Garfagnini and Strulovici 2016; Carnehl and Schneider 2025; Callander, Lambert and Matouschek, forthcoming).

Worst-case versions replace the prior with a Lipschitz bound (Malladi 2025; Banchio and Malladi 2025). Hodgson and Lewis (2025) estimate a Gaussian-process search model on consumer clickstream data.

Mean-reverting landscapes

A Brownian landscape drifts without bound away from known territory. An Ornstein–Uhlenbeck landscape reverts, so performance far from what is known regresses to an average. Its covariance is the exponential kernel, the Matérn kernel with $\nu = 1/2$ (Rasmussen and Williams 2006).

In the economics literature, Aybas and Callander (2025) allow Itô-diffusion landscapes including the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck case, in a one-shot cheap-talk game. Bardhi (2024) proves that the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck kernel is the only powered-exponential covariance with the Markov property.

The same kernel appears in other fields. Weinberger (1990) measures the ruggedness of a fitness landscape by the exponential decay of a random walk's autocorrelation. In evolutionary biology the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process models a trait held near an optimum (Hansen 1997; Butler and King 2004). Radio shadowing has exponential spatial correlation (Gudmundson 1991).

Optimization with large budgets

In optimization the same object appears with many evaluations. Calvin (1997) derives average-case convergence rates under Wiener measure. Grill, Valko and Munos (2018) approximate the maximum of a Brownian path, and Grosse, Zhang and Hennig (2023) extend this to Gaussian-process samples with regret rates.

Alabert and Caballero (2018) compute the law of the minimum of a conditioned Brownian bridge to compare fixed sampling designs. The probabilistic line search of Mahsereci and Hennig (2017) is a Gaussian-process line search for stochastic gradient descent, built on gradient observations.

Bayesian optimization

Acquisition rules in Bayesian optimization differ along two lines (Shahriari et al. 2016). One is myopic against lookahead. Probability of improvement (Kushner 1964), expected improvement (Mockus et al. 1978; Jones et al. 1998), the upper confidence bound (Srinivas et al. 2010) and entropy search (Hennig and Schuler 2012; Wang and Jegelka 2017) are one-step rules.

Multi-step rules exist in approximate form:

  • the observation that one-step rules are suboptimal under a genuine budget (Ginsbourger and Le Riche 2010);
  • finite-budget optimization as an approximate dynamic program (Lam, Willcox and Wolpert 2016);
  • two-step lookahead (Wu and Frazier 2019) and the full lookahead tree (Jiang et al. 2020).

The other line is the payoff: cumulative regret, or the value of a final recommendation. The knowledge gradient (Frazier, Powell and Dayanik 2009) scores the final recommendation. With one informative trial before a terminal recommendation it is optimal by construction.

Where few-shot search sits

The grass problem has a fixed budget of three evaluations, pays only at the final placement, and uses a mean-reverting landscape. In the taxonomy above it is lookahead with a terminal recommendation, the knowledge-gradient corner. On the exponentiated Ornstein–Uhlenbeck landscape that policy is characterized exactly: the final placement in closed form, the opening trial by one-dimensional quadrature.

Larger budgets, other objectives and several dimensions are open, and are listed on the home page.

Practice and experiment

Across four randomized trials covering 759 firms, 59% never pivoted radically during the study, 24% pivoted once and 17% more than once (Camuffo et al. 2024; Camuffo et al. 2020). Practitioners call the fork pivot or persevere (Ries 2011).

Glick and Myers (2015) tested the one-evaluation problem in the lab. Subjects respond to the Brownian structure but modify too much when the variance is high.
